Case Study

The tools I built—and deliberately retired

Content strategists talk a lot about AI. I wanted receipts. So I designed and shipped two working prototypes—a crawler that audits information architecture and a copy critique engine—then retired their public endpoints once the learning objective was complete.

The premise

"AI content architecture" should come with proof

My positioning says AI content architecture. It's easy to put those words on a website; it's harder to demonstrate them. The most convincing demonstration I could think of was to take the two things I do professionally — audit content systems and critique copy — and build working AI tools that do them, in public, for anyone who typed in a URL.

The other reason: you don't really understand AI-assisted content tooling until you've had to decide what the model should be trusted with, what should stay deterministic, and what to do when a stranger feeds your crawler the address of your cloud provider's metadata service.

Building the tools taught me the thing I now tell everyone: AI product work is content strategy work. The prompts are editorial standards. The scoring rubric is a voice decision. "Don't fabricate issues" is governance.

The tools

An IA auditor and a copy clinic

CSX Audit takes a URL and returns a content strategy audit. It crawls the site breadth-first (respecting robots.txt, and honest enough to warn you when a JavaScript-rendered app is hiding its content), builds a page-and-link graph, computes information architecture metrics, and then layers AI analysis on top — ending in an executive summary, prioritized recommendations, and quick wins.

Copy Clinic takes up to 1,500 characters of UX copy and returns a critique through a content strategy lens: what's working, what isn't (with a no-hedging rule), a rewrite, and a one-sentence strategic insight the copy reveals. The persona in the prompt is me on a good day: specific, direct, and required to quote your actual words back to you.

The interesting part

The hard problems were content problems

The serverless plumbing was work, but the decisions that made the tools good were editorial. The audit prompts carry explicit standards: identify real problems; if the IA is solid, return fewer findings with lower severity; don't fabricate issues. The gap analysis has an evidence rule — every claimed gap must trace to a page that raises the question without answering it, or to a clear expectation for that type of site. That's the same discipline I'd demand from a human auditor, written down where the model can't ignore it.

Even the scoring is an editorial position. A site with no findings scores 88, not 100 — a ten-page crawl can't prove perfection, and the tool shouldn't pretend otherwise. Repeated findings of the same severity hit diminishing returns, because the fifth missing CTA is the same insight as the second. None of that came from an engineering requirement. It came from having done real audits and knowing where they go wrong.

The unglamorous parts

Letting strangers use your tools without regretting it

A public tool that fetches arbitrary URLs and spends real API money on every request is a liability with a nice interface. Most of the code that isn't content strategy is there to keep the tools safe and solvent:

Both tools ran live on this site. They're currently offline — every audit spends real API money, and a portfolio doesn't need an unattended bill — but the code stays in the repo, and turning them back on is a config change, not a rebuild.

What survives either way is the point they were built to make: the craft I bring to a content system — standards, evidence, honest limits, judgment about what to automate — is exactly the craft AI products need. I didn't read that in a think piece. I shipped it.
